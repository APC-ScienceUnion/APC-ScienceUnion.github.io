A Proof of the Water-Filling Theorem

Problem Statement

  The water-filling theorem solves a fundamental problem in information theory: how should power be allocated across AWGN channels to maximize their total capacity? Suppose there are KKK parallel AWGN channels with mutually independent noise processes and noise powers σ12,σ22,⋅⋅⋅,σK2σ^2_1,σ^2_2,··· ,σ^2_Kσ12​,σ22​,⋅⋅⋅,σK2​. Given a total power budget PPP, we seek the allocation that maximizes the combined capacity of the KKK channels.

  As an optimization problem, this becomes:

max⁡p1,…,pK∑k=1Klog⁡2(1+pkσk2)s.t.∑k=1Kpk≤Ppk≥0,   k=1,2,…,K(1.1)\begin{aligned} &\max_{p_1, \dots , p_K}\sum^{K}_{k=1} \log_2 \left(1 + \frac{p_k}{\sigma ^2_k}\right)\\ \mathrm{s.t.}&\sum^K_{k = 1}p_k \le P\\ &p_k \ge 0, \ \ \ k = 1, 2, \dots , K\end{aligned} \tag{1.1}s.t.​p1​,…,pK​max​k=1∑K​log2​(1+σk2​pk​​)k=1∑K​pk​≤Ppk​≥0,   k=1,2,…,K​(1.1)

An Intuitive Proof

  We begin with a relatively intuitive derivation.

  At first, it may seem best to assign all available power to the channel with the least noise. But a channel’s capacity is Ck=log⁡2(1+pk/σk2)C_k = \log_2 (1 + p_k / σ^2_k)Ck​=log2​(1+pk​/σk2​), so the marginal gain in capacity decreases as its allocated power increases. Each additional increment of power should therefore go to the channel that currently provides the largest marginal gain.

  Assume an initial allocation p1(0),p2(0),…,pK(0)p^{(0)}_1 ,p^{(0)}_2 , \dots ,p^{(0)}_Kp1(0)​,p2(0)​,…,pK(0)​ satisfying:

∑k=1Kpk(0)<P,   pk(0)≥0,   k=1,2,…,K\sum^K_{k = 1}p^{(0)}_k < P, \ \ \ p^{(0)}_{k} \ge 0, \ \ \ k = 1, 2, \dots , Kk=1∑K​pk(0)​<P,   pk(0)​≥0,   k=1,2,…,K

  Clearly, p1(0)=p2(0)=⋯=pK(0)=0p^{(0)}_1 = p^{(0)}_2 = \dots = p^{(0)}_K = 0p1(0)​=p2(0)​=⋯=pK(0)​=0 is a valid starting point. Let a positive quantity (P−∑k=1Kpk(0))≥δ>0(P - \sum^K_{k=1}p^{(0)}_k) \ge \delta > 0(P−∑k=1K​pk(0)​)≥δ>0 be the next increment of power to allocate. Assigning δ\deltaδ to channel kkk increases its capacity by:

ΔC=log⁡2(1+pk(0)+δσk2)−log⁡2(1+pk(0)σk2)=log⁡2(1+δpk(0)+σk2)(2.1)\Delta C = \log_2 \left( 1 + \frac{p^{(0)}_k + \delta}{\sigma^2_k}\right) - \log_2 \left(1 + \frac{p^{(0)}_k}{\sigma^2_k}\right) = \log_2 \left(1 + \frac{\delta}{p^{(0)}_k + \sigma^2_k}\right)\tag{2.1}ΔC=log2​(1+σk2​pk(0)​+δ​)−log2​(1+σk2​pk(0)​​)=log2​(1+pk(0)​+σk2​δ​)(2.1)

  The increment should plainly go to the channel with the smallest pk(0)+σk2p^{(0)}_k + \sigma^2_kpk(0)​+σk2​. If δ\deltaδ is made arbitrarily small at each step, then once all the power has been allocated, every active channel will have the same value of σk2+pk\sigma^2_k +p_kσk2​+pk​. Channels whose noise power σk2σ^2_kσk2​ exceeds this level receive no power. Thus:

pk=max⁡{0,p∗−σk2}(2.2)p_k = \max \{0, p^* - \sigma^2_k\}\tag{2.2}pk​=max{0,p∗−σk2​}(2.2)

  Here p⋆p^⋆p⋆ is chosen so that ∑k=1Kpk=P\sum^K_{k = 1}p_k = P∑k=1K​pk​=P.

  Consider any allocation for which pi+σi2>pj+σj2p_i + \sigma^2_i > p_j + \sigma^2_jpi​+σi2​>pj​+σj2​, and set δ=min⁡{pi,(pi+σi2−pj−σj2)/2}\delta = \min\{p_i, (p_i + \sigma^2_i - p_j - \sigma^2_j)/2\}δ=min{pi​,(pi​+σi2​−pj​−σj2​)/2}. Beginning with pi(0)=pi−δ,pj(0)=pjp^{(0)}_i = p_i - \delta, p^{(0)}_j = p_jpi(0)​=pi​−δ,pj(0)​=pj​, the argument above shows that assigning δ\deltaδ to channel jjj produces more capacity than assigning it to channel iii. After this reallocation, either pi+σi2=pj+σj2p_i + \sigma^2_i = p_j + \sigma^2_jpi​+σi2​=pj​+σj2​ or pi=0p_i = 0pi​=0; in the latter case, σi2≥pj+σj2\sigma_i^2 \ge p_j + \sigma^2_jσi2​≥pj​+σj2​.

  Rewrite (1.1)(1.1)(1.1) as:

  Without loss of generality, suppose σ12≤σ22≤⋯≤σK2\sigma^2_1 \le \sigma^2_2 \le \dots \le \sigma^2_Kσ12​≤σ22​≤⋯≤σK2​. Because the pkp_kpk​ cannot all vanish, at least one pkp_kpk​ is positive, and its corresponding λkλ_kλk​ is zero. For channels with pk>0,λk=0p_k > 0, λ_k = 0pk​>0,λk​=0:

  Because σk2σ^2_kσk2​ increases monotonically:

  Why is this called “water-filling”? Picture σ2(f)σ^2 (f)σ2(f) as the bottom of a bowl and the available power as water poured into it.
