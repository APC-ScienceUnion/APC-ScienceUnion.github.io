Does Pi Always Equal 3.14…?

Created2023-04-24|Updated2026-08-30
|Word count:2.4k|Reading time:15min|Post View:

Author: ΔδDelta\Delta\delta DeltaΔδDelta
Reviewer: Baiyan

  Years ago, a science fiction story titled “Pythagoras: 2.013” took the internet by storm, and people still remember it today. One passage near the end invites the reader to speculate:

“Do not judge their intelligence from our point of view. Perhaps our own civilization, too, exists within some larger region of distorted spacetime. Don’t you find the value of pi, 3.1416, an exceedingly strange number?”

  The story has plenty of flaws, but they do not diminish its literary value. It genuinely inspired many people to study science and mathematics. Even today, it offers a useful starting point for a short exploration of the value of pi.

  So let us return to that evocative line: is pi, π=3.1415926…\pi = 3.1415926 \dotsπ=3.1415926…, really such a strange number? Does it have that value because spacetime is distorted?

  The answer to both questions is no. We should not fault an irrational number on aesthetic grounds merely because we cannot find a pattern in it. That would hardly be fair. Can anyone reduce every work of art to a single pattern? (Voice offstage: AI ART!!! Machines can draw only because we found the “pattern” behind art, right?) But the parameters that define those AIs do not follow a pattern either, do they? It is 3202; surely everyone knows by now that neural-network algorithms are black boxes?

  The second question is even more far-fetched. Our familiar 3.14…3.14\dots3.14… comes from a circle in a plane. What kind of plane? The mathematical two-dimensional Euclidean plane. To define it, we need only form ordered pairs (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) of real numbers and specify a formula for the “distance” between them. Spacetime can twist however it likes; that affects only a physical “plane.” A tabletop that looks flat in everyday life, for instance, is actually curved, with spacetime curvature making it appear flat, and so on. A mathematical plane, however, is defined abstractly. Nothing in physical reality can alter a mathematical concept.

  As the saying goes, the person who tied the bell must untie it. If we truly want to change pi, we have to change a mathematical definition. We will keep the rest of the discussion in the real plane: each point is specified by exactly two real numbers. So what can we still change?

  Exactly: we still have to specify how to calculate “distance.” In the second year of graduate school, we encountered the famous distance formula for two points (x1,y1),(x2,y2)(x_1, y_1), (x_2, y_2)(x1​,y1​),(x2​,y2​):

d2=((x1−x2)2+(y1−y2)2)12.d_2 = ((x_1 - x_2)^2 + (y_1 - y_2)^2 )^{\frac{1}{2}}.d2​=((x1​−x2​)2+(y1​−y2​)2)21​.

  Meanwhile, our teachers had already introduced the Manhattan distance, widely used in computing, back in first grade: add the vertical difference between the points to their horizontal difference. In symbols:

d1=∣x1−x2∣+∣y1−y2∣.d_1 = |x_1 - x_2| + |y_1 - y_2|.d1​=∣x1​−x2​∣+∣y1​−y2​∣.

  Still unconvinced by the claim that irregularity is no aesthetic failing, you immediately go looking for an elegant pattern. Brimming with confidence, you propose the following distance formula in terms of ppp:

dp=(∣x1−x2∣p+∣y1−y2∣p)1p.d_p = (|x_1 - x_2|^p + |y_1 - y_2|^p )^{\frac{1}{p}}.dp​=(∣x1​−x2​∣p+∣y1​−y2​∣p)p1​.

  Faced with a reckless conclusion built from equal parts guesswork and bravado, I can only say…:

  That’s right: this was the lesson you slept through in your final year of kindergarten. A function (or functional) that measures distance this way is called a “ppp-norm.”

  Your next question is obvious: how can this distance formula change pi? Friends, let us turn our attention to that circle in the distance.

  Once we change the distance formula, the first thing to change is the shape of the so-called “circle.” Put its center at the origin and let its radius be 111. Its equation is then:

Cp:(∣x∣p+∣y∣p)1p=1.C_p : (|x|^p + |y|^p )^{\frac{1}{p}} = 1.Cp​:(∣x∣p+∣y∣p)p1​=1.

  What shape does the “circle” take under the familiar Manhattan distance?

  It becomes a square rotated by 45°45\degree45°! Under this definition of distance, its circumference is C1=a×(1+1)=8C_1 = a \times (1 + 1) = 8C1​=a×(1+1)=8, so pi is π1=C12=4\pi _1 = \frac{C_1}{2} = 4π1​=2C1​​=4! Not only is it different from the usual 3.14…3.14\dots3.14…; it is no longer irrational at all. It has become an integer. We have now answered the question in the title: pi does not always equal 3.14…3.14\dots3.14….

  Next, we would like to know the value of pi under other norms. The Manhattan distance above corresponds to p=1p = 1p=1, but can ppp be less than 111? Before answering, recall a bit of geometry we abandoned years ago: the sum of two sides of a triangle is greater than the third side. Those side lengths are measured with the p=2p = 2p=2 norm, but the familiar rule remains true whenever p>1p > 1p>1. At p=1p = 1p=1, the sum of two sides equals the third, and in the spirit of “close enough,” we will still let the 111-norm join the party. But p∈(0,1)p\in (0,1)p∈(0,1) goes a little too far: the sum of two sides would be shorter than the third. Are you telling me that walking straight to my destination is less convenient than taking a long detour? A proper norm therefore requires p≥1p ≥ 1p≥1; that is, it must satisfy the triangle inequality f(x+y)≥f(x)+f(y)f(x + y) ≥ f(x) + f(y)f(x+y)≥f(x)+f(y).

  In other words, the distance under the ∞\infty∞-norm is whichever is greater, the vertical difference or the horizontal difference. We take the larger of ∣x1−x2∣,∣y1−y2∣|x1 − x2|, |y1 − y2|∣x1−x2∣,∣y1−y2∣ as the distance. The circle therefore has the equation

  Wait! Did you just say 4?! Pi is 444 under both the 111-norm and the ∞\infty∞-norm, but in familiar Euclidean space, the 222-norm case, pi is 3.14⋯<43.14\dots < 43.14⋯<4. As ppp varies in the ppp-norm, pi becomes a function of ppp, namely π(p)\pi (p)π(p).   I have to admit that Li Yunlong was right: you guessed correctly again. Better yet, the minimum occurs exactly at p=2p = 2p=2, where pi has its familiar value, 3.1415926…3.1415926\dots3.1415926….
