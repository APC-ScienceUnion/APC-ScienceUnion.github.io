SpaceX Rocket Launches the First Privately Funded Lunar Lander

Created2019-02-22|Updated2026-08-24
|Word count:826|Reading time:5min|Post View:

Author: A Millisecond of Eternity

  At 9:45 a.m. Beijing time on February 22, 2019, just as the Moon rose above the horizon, a Falcon 9 rocket began its journey toward it.

  On the evening of February 21, 2019, a previously flown SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket successfully launched an Israeli lunar lander and an Indonesian communications satellite from Cape Canaveral Air Force Station in the United States. After placing both payloads into orbit, Falcon 9’s first stage returned to Earth and landed successfully on the uncrewed vessel Of Course I Still Love You in the Atlantic Ocean.

  Although the mission’s primary payload was Indonesia’s Nusantara Satu satellite, the small lunar lander traveling as a secondary payload stole the spotlight. It became not only Israel’s first spacecraft to venture beyond Earth orbit, but also the first privately funded lunar lander.

Concept art of the Beresheet lander on the lunar surface | Credit: SpaceIL

  Today’s launch marks an important moment for Israel. Apart from several communications and Earth-observation satellites, the country has never launched a mission beyond Earth orbit.

  The lander is named Beresheet—Hebrew for “in the beginning.” It was built by SpaceIL, an Israeli nonprofit that entered the mission in 2011 as a contestant in the Google Lunar X Prize. The international competition offered 30 million US dollars to any privately funded team that could land a robotic spacecraft on the Moon. If no team met the deadline, the competition would have no winner.

  “Our vision is to show the world the best that Israel has to offer,” SpaceIL donor Sylvan Adams said at a briefing in Orlando, Florida, on February 20, 2019. “Israel is about to become the fourth country to soft-land on the Moon. That is an extraordinary achievement.”

Beresheet will orbit Earth repeatedly for about 27 days to build the momentum it needs.

  Beresheet will take seven weeks to reach the Moon. Before it arrives, it will circle Earth several times, using Earth’s gravity to gain the momentum needed to reach its destination. If Beresheet makes a successful soft landing, Israel will become the fourth country to achieve a soft landing on the Moon, following the United States, Russia, and China. In 1966, the Soviet spacecraft Luna 9 made the first soft landing on the Moon. Later that year, NASA’s Surveyor 1 landed on the lunar surface. China’s Chang’e 3 lander and Yutu rover joined them in 2013.

  When Beresheet approaches the lunar surface, it will aim to land in the region known as Mare Tranquillitatis, SpaceIL spacecraft program manager Yigal Harel said at the briefing. The spacecraft, however, will be able to operate for only about two days before running out of power. During that time, it will study the Moon’s magnetic field and photograph the lunar surface.

  Even after those two days, the defunct spacecraft will not be entirely useless. Beresheet carries a laser retroreflector, a mirror-like device that requires no power and enables space-to-ground communication through NASA’s Deep Space Network (DSN). NASA provided the device for the mission, and the agreement will allow the startup to use the DSN for its lunar mission.

  The reflector is not the only payload aboard Beresheet designed to endure. SpaceIL also built a time capsule filled with digital files about the spacecraft and a range of Israeli mementos. They include the Hebrew Bible, Hebrew songs, artwork created by Israeli children, and photographs of Ilan Ramon, Israel’s first and only astronaut. Inside the time capsule is a vast digital database known as the “Lunar Library,” a project of the nonprofit Arch Mission Foundation. “The library holds millions of documents from around the world, including many different dictionaries and encyclopedias, as well as three laser-etched coins,” said SpaceIL cofounder Yonatan Winetraub. “It will be the most information-dense artifact ever to reach the Moon, and it will remain there in 2019 so that future generations can see what life on Earth was like.”

This small coin is part of the time capsule aboard Beresheet. The entire Hebrew Bible is laser-etched onto its surface. | Credits: Hanneke Weitering/Space.com

  Beresheet and Nusantara Satu were launched on a Falcon 9 booster that had already flown two separate missions to space, making this its third successful launch and landing. SpaceX had first flown and recovered the same first-stage booster for a third time during the SSO-A mission in December 2018; the Nusantara Satu mission was the company’s second time reaching that milestone. The booster may also be refurbished for a historic fourth flight. Reusing rocket boosters can drastically reduce the cost of spaceflight, a central goal of SpaceX.

Note: Beresheet attempted to land on April 11, 2019, but lost contact after its main engine failed during the final stage of descent. It ultimately crashed on the lunar surface in the early hours of that day, Beijing time.
