# The Remarkable History of the Wok (Part I)

&emsp;&emsp;Of all the tools in the kitchen, the wok is king: if it is not frying, it is braising. Three days to master an electric cooker, three years for the ladle, ten years to hone a cleaver, and a lifetime at the iron wok.<br> Food goes in, wine is poured, and the whole village waits to be served. The fire starts, the spatula rises, and one wok can feed two tables. The dishes arrive, chopsticks are laid out, and friends and family tuck in.<br> Today, almost every Chinese household has a pitch-black wok. As its Chinese name suggests, its main purpose is stir-frying. Stir-frying is arguably the dominant technique in Chinese cooking today. From ordinary roadside restaurants to five-star hotels, most dishes are prepared either by stir-frying or by a method derived from it. The emergence of “stir-frying” 1,500 years ago laid the foundation for the defining character of later Chinese cuisine, and it now satisfies countless hungry diners.

&emsp;&emsp;As the saying goes, “A wok is forever; one will be passed down through the ages.” So just how far back does its history go?

&emsp;&emsp;We must begin with the wok's elder brother, the iron pot. Both are made of iron, but one came first: the iron pot was the first-generation product, and the wok appeared only after that product was improved and upgraded. It is therefore only fair to call the iron pot the elder brother. Its own history begins with a distant relative, the bronze pot. Bronze vessels were the first metal goods that humans used on a large scale; only after people made bronze pots did the very idea of a pot take shape.

&emsp;&emsp;What? You have never heard of a bronze pot? You must at least recognize a *ding*. Yes, the *ding* was the earliest slow-acting-suicide cooking utensil, and it too is a kind of pot.

&emsp;&emsp;Anyone with a basic knowledge of history probably knows that metal goods in the Zhou dynasty were made of bronze. By the Spring and Autumn and Warring States periods, however, things had changed: anything that could be made of metal was being made of iron.

&emsp;&emsp;So perhaps, during the Spring and Autumn and Warring States periods, people were already hammering out iron versions of the old bronze pots. A conservative estimate therefore places the appearance of the iron pot in that period.

# The Remarkable History of the Wok—Steaming, Boiling, and Roasting in the Tang; Every Method in the Song (Part I)

&emsp;&emsp;[<strong>Last time</strong>](http://mp.weixin.qq.com/s?__biz=MzU2Njc3MTM4Ng==&mid=2247485144&idx=1&sn=c5f5cd2b67b5266dc4061515779eca89&chksm=fca62d0dcbd1a41b51ae21939ec5ae6de1fb5dfb9dca3b2cae855484ae3889505ac6174c9d47&scene=21#wechat_redirect), we saw that a prototype wok and scrambled eggs cooked in it had already appeared during the Northern and Southern dynasties. Does that mean woks were everywhere by the Tang dynasty?

&emsp;&emsp;History says otherwise. In the Tang, cooking still revolved around the same old trio: steaming, boiling, and roasting.

&emsp;&emsp;The *Shuowen Jiezi* records: “A *ding*, with three legs and two handles, is the precious vessel in which the five flavors are blended.”

&emsp;&emsp;The picture makes it obvious that the *ding* was a tool for boiling and steaming. The method was essentially “throw it all in one big pot”: put every ingredient in at once, cover it, and let it cook.

&emsp;&emsp;① For one thing, it was extremely convenient. Can you light a fire? Can you add water? Can you put food in? Yes? Then you are done. How convenient is that?

&emsp;&emsp;② People at the time did not demand much from their food. (You were lucky to have anything to eat; why be picky...?)

&emsp;&emsp;As for whether it tasted good...... Sorry, that was outside the scope of consideration...

&emsp;&emsp;But do not look down on steaming and boiling just because the food did not taste good, you fool. Even today, Western cooks rarely use “steaming.” Take France, whose cuisine is famous worldwide: some say its chefs do not even have a concept of “steaming.”

&emsp;&emsp;Now let us turn to roasting. The Tang campaigns in the north brought large quantities of northern mutton into the empire, along with the soul of barbecue—cumin—from the Middle East. So people in the Tang could eat lamb skewers, and lamb was not the only meat they roasted.

&emsp;&emsp;The Tang physician Jiu Yin (who may have spent all day hanging around barbecue stalls) recorded several grilled dishes in the *Shiyi Xinjian*: mandarin duck, wild boar, eel, myna, and yellow hen.

&emsp;&emsp;Thus, even if the Tang had woks, there was little use for them. They existed, but only in very small numbers. Woks did not begin to grow more common until the transition from the Tang to the Song.

&emsp;&emsp;Salt and iron were often state monopolies in imperial China, and the Song was no exception. Mining and iron smelting were overseen by the government: local mine owners handled extraction, while a “professional team” took care of smelting.

&emsp;&emsp;The Baoyuan Bureau was originally a mint, the equivalent of a modern coin factory. Ancient currency was made of metal, but everyday pots, bowls, ladles, basins, hoes, rakes, shovels, and the like also required metal. Rather than trouble a second office, the state mint made these farm tools too. Its directors must have found the situation galling: a dignified national mint reduced to casting farm implements!?

&emsp;&emsp;All right, back to the point. Someone is bound to complain: over the three hundred years between the Tang and Song, were those craftspeople doing nothing but eating? They could not even make something as simple as a wok. Put me in charge and the Tang would be eating stir-fries in minutes. (Insert doge face.)

&emsp;&emsp;Hey, do not complain that this was slow. A wok “midwife” was nagging away the entire time, pushing for woks to be manufactured and used on a large scale and spurring the emergence of the new cooking method called “stir-frying.”

&emsp;&emsp;There was more than one of these nags: there were two. One had been chattering without pause from the Han dynasty all the way to the Song, nearly a thousand years. The other began during the Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms period.
